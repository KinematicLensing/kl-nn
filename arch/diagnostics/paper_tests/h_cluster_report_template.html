Cluster Substructure Test

Can likelihood stacking of our NPE posteriors see a subhalo with 30% of the host's mass, at the source density a DESI-like KL survey would have? This page covers the setup, how every step of the analysis works, and the validation of the stacking estimator on synthetic data. The run on the real NPE posteriors is ready but not yet analysed.

Data generated (141 × 2 scenarios) NPE posteriors cached Stacking estimator validated Subhalo sits inside the central polar cell NPE analysis · halo fit · κ tuning: next

Summary

  • The cell-stacking estimator is unbiased and its error bars are right, to within 7%. Over 30 synthetic noise realizations the pulls average 0.00–0.03 in size with a spread of 0.93–0.94, in both scenarios and both cell layouts.
  • Cells holding only 1–2 galaxies are slightly shrunk toward zero, by about 0.005. That is the same shrinkage the constant-shear benchmark shows at N = 1–2. Cells with 3 or more galaxies are unbiased to better than 0.002. Sparse cells also carry the largest error bars, so they already count for less.
  • At this density the subhalo is not visible in a single synthetic realization, as the S/N forecast (best case ≈ 2) predicted. Both κ maps recover the main halo.
  • A design issue to fix: the central polar cell needs a 0.57 Mpc radius to hold about 5 galaxies, so the subhalo at 0.5 Mpc falls inside it and cannot be separated from the main halo. I propose moving the subhalo to about 1 Mpc, where the forecast S/N is the same.

How the analysis works

1. The cluster and the two scenarios

One cluster at zl = 0.13, where the lensing efficiency for sources at z = 0.3 peaks. Two shear fields are built from the same background galaxies:

  • main: one NFW halo at the origin;
  • sub: the same halo plus an NFW subhalo with 30% of its mass, placed on the x axis.

Both come from GalSim's NFWHalo. The halos are added in shear γ and convergence κ, and only then converted to the reduced shear g = γ/(1 − κ) that galaxies respond to. Adding reduced shears directly would be wrong.

Galaxies are placed uniformly at random. Each takes the parameters of a random Xu3 measured-axis test galaxy: Rh ≥ 1″, vcirc from the TF relation, catalog image and Hα S/N. Only its two shear components change. Positions, parameters, fiber-targeting seeds and the NPE noise draws are shared between the scenarios, so the two datasets differ only through the shear.

2. One likelihood per galaxy

For each galaxy the NPE gives 104 posterior samples of all nine parameters, reweighted to the Tully–Fisher prior. Its shear likelihood is the shear posterior divided by the prior it was trained under:

Li(g) ∝ q(g | datai) / πtrain(g), with πtrain uniform on |gk| < 0.1

We turn the samples into a smooth density with a kernel density estimate and store log Li on a 401 × 401 grid in (g1, g2). The settings match the constant-shear stacking: Scott bandwidth, reflection at the box edges, and a small floor so one galaxy cannot veto a shear.

Every later step reads these grids. Evaluating a galaxy's likelihood at any shear is then one bilinear interpolation.

3. Cells and stacking

Galaxies are grouped into cells holding about 5 galaxies on average. Every non-empty cell is kept.

  • Polar cells: a central disk, then 4 log-spaced annuli out to 3 Mpc. Each annulus is cut into as many sectors as keep about 5 galaxies per cell, and the subhalo direction falls at a sector centre. These cells are stacked in tangential/cross components (gt, g×) about the main-halo centre, so a subhalo would show as extra gt or non-zero g×.
  • Cartesian cells: squares of 0.98 Mpc, stacked in (g1, g2).

Within a cell we look for one common shear G and multiply the galaxies' likelihoods. For polar cells each galaxy's likelihood is first rotated into the frame at its own position angle:

p(G | cell) ∝ ∏i ∈ cell Li(RiT G), uniform prior on |G| < 0.1

The stacked estimate is the mean of p(G), and its error bar σ is the standard deviation.

What the stack should be compared with. The shear varies inside a cell, so there is no single "true" cell shear. For Gaussian likelihoods, the product above returns a precision-weighted mean of the galaxies' true shears:

gref = (Σi Fi)−1 Σi Fi gi,true, Fi = inverse covariance of Li in the cell frame

We use that as the reference. The weights are matrices, not numbers, because KL pins one shear direction much more tightly than the other. A plain average of the true shears would be the wrong target.

4. Halo fit (built, not yet run on the real data)

Instead of comparing cells with a reference, this step fits a shear-field model directly to the galaxies. Each galaxy's likelihood is evaluated at the shear the model predicts at its own position:

L(θ) = ∏i Li( gmodel(xi; θ) )

This is the natural extension of constant-shear stacking to a varying shear field, in the spirit of Bernstein & Armstrong (2014). There is no binning and no reference shear to define.

  • θ is log10 Mmain, plus log10 Msub in the subhalo model.
  • Concentrations follow Duffy+08, and both centres are fixed at their true positions.
  • Priors are wide and log-uniform: Mmain from 1013.5 to 1016 M☉ and Msub from 1012 to 1015.5 M☉.
  • Nautilus samples both models (main-only and main + sub) for both datasets. Their evidences give a Bayes factor for the subhalo, quoted as a guide, not as a detection claim.

5. κ maps: Kaiser–Squires and our likelihood-based map

κ (projected mass) and γ (shear) are second derivatives of the same lensing potential, so the shear map is fixed by the κ map. In Fourier space one kernel links them, D(k) = (k1² − k2² + 2i k1k2)/k². D only rotates each mode and never changes its size.

Kaiser–Squires (standard). This goes from shear to κ, using κ̂ = D* γ̂:

  1. put the stacked Cartesian-cell shears on their grid, with empty cells set to zero and the grid zero-padded to twice its size;
  2. Fourier transform, multiply by the inverse kernel D*, and transform back;
  3. smooth by 0.7 cell.

It is fast, but it treats every cell the same regardless of how many galaxies it holds, assumes g ≈ γ, and only ever sees cell averages.

Likelihood-based (ours). This goes the other way, from κ to shear, and compares with the data:

  1. the unknowns are κ on a 12 × 12 grid of 0.5 Mpc pixels;
  2. for a trial κ map, compute γ with the forward kernel, γ̂ = D κ̂, on a zero-padded grid, which means assuming κ = 0 outside the field;
  3. interpolate γ and κ to each galaxy's exact position and form gi = γi/(1 − κi);
  4. score the map with Σi log Li(gi), the same per-galaxy likelihoods as above, so precise galaxies pull harder, direction by direction;
  5. add a Gaussian prior that penalises large κ (σκ = 0.1) and differences between neighbouring pixels. This replaces the smoothing step, much like a Wiener filter;
  6. find the most probable map with a gradient optimiser (L-BFGS in PyTorch).

The residual map is the likelihood map of the with-subhalo data minus the κ of the best-fitting main-halo-only model. A subhalo should appear as a positive blob at its position.

The same scheme has a long history: Bridle+98 and Marshall+02 (maximum-entropy reconstruction from per-galaxy reduced-shear likelihoods), and Schneider+17 (probabilistic mass mapping). The new ingredient is the data term, each galaxy's learned KL likelihood. One caveat: the prior pulls κ toward zero, so peaks come out lower than the truth. Compare maps with the truth through the same pipeline; don't read κ values off at face value.

6. How the estimator was validated

Before trusting the NPE run, we replace every galaxy's likelihood with a Gaussian whose covariance is that galaxy's real NPE shear covariance, taken from its Xu3 source galaxy. It is centred on a noisy copy of its true shear, with the same noise in both scenarios. The truth is then known exactly, and the whole chain (KDE grids, cells, stacking, reference) runs unchanged. We repeat this for 30 independent noise realizations of the same 141 galaxies.

What a "pull" is

For each cell, the pull is how far the stacked shear lands from the reference, measured in units of the cell's own error bar:

pull = (stacked − reference) / σ

If the estimator is unbiased and its error bars are honest, pulls behave like draws from a standard normal distribution:

  • Mean ≈ 0. A clearly non-zero mean means a bias: the stack lands systematically on one side.
  • Spread (standard deviation) ≈ 1. Above 1 means the error bars are too small (overconfident); below 1 means they are too large (conservative).

With 29 cells in one realization, the mean pull scatters by about 1/√29 ≈ 0.19 from noise alone. That is why we averaged over 30 realizations.

One realization: cells and maps

A single synthetic realization, analysed exactly as the NPE data will be. With 29 polar cells the fitted slopes are noisy (m = 0.75 ± 0.35 and 0.64 ± 0.27 for polar gt). The cell χ² is 28.9 and 26.7 for 27 degrees of freedom, and 34.7 and 33.9 for 35 in the Cartesian cells, so the scatter matches the error bars. The large slope uncertainty comes from the narrow range of cell shears (0.005–0.035), which makes slope and offset degenerate. The validation above is the meaningful test of bias.

Next steps

  1. Move the subhalo out of the central cell. I'd put it at 1 Mpc, which the S/N forecast says costs nothing, regenerate the 141 + 141 galaxies (about 15 minutes) and rerun the synthetic check.
  2. Tune the likelihood map on synthetic data only: try 0.75 Mpc pixels and a stronger smoothness prior, choose one setting, then apply it once to the NPE data.
  3. Run the full analysis on the NPE posteriors: cells, bias fits (linear and cubic), Nautilus halo fits and κ maps.
  4. If the subhalo is still not visible, repeat at sample-1 density (about 620 galaxies, cells of about 0.43 Mpc, forecast S/N ≈ 4).
